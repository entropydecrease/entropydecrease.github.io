How to Raise Money 如何融资

September 2013

2013年9月

Most startups that raise money do it more than once. A typical trajectory might be (1) to get started with a few tens of thousands from something like Y Combinator or individual angels, then (2) raise a few hundred thousand to a few million to build the company, and then (3) once the company is clearly succeeding, raise one or more later rounds to accelerate growth. Reality can be messier. Some companies raise money twice in phase 2. Others skip phase 1 and go straight to phase 2. And at Y Combinator we get an increasing number of companies that have already raised amounts in the hundreds of thousands. But the three phase path is at least the one about which individual startups’ paths oscillate. This essay focuses on phase 2 fundraising. That’s the type the startups we fund are doing on Demo Day, and this essay is the advice we give them.

大多数融资的初创公司都不止融资一次。一个典型的轨迹可能是：

（1）从 Y Combinator 或个人天使等渠道获得几万美元起步； （2）融资几十万到几百万美元来建立公司； （3）一旦公司明显成功，再融资一轮或多轮后续融资来加速增长。现实可能更复杂。

有些公司在第二阶段融资两次。有些跳过第一阶段直接进入第二阶段。在 Y Combinator，我们看到的已经融资数十万美元的公司越来越多。但三阶段路径至少是各个初创公司路径波动的基准。本文聚焦于第二阶段融资。这正是我们资助的公司在 Demo Day 上做的事情，而本文就是我们给他们的建议。

Forces

力量

Fundraising is hard in both senses: hard like lifting a heavy weight, and hard like solving a puzzle. It’s hard like lifting a weight because it’s intrinsically hard to convince people to part with large sums of money. That problem is irreducible; it should be hard. But much of the other kind of difficulty can be eliminated. Fundraising only seems a puzzle because it’s an alien world to most founders, and I hope to fix that by supplying a map through it.

融资在两种意义上都很困难：像举重一样困难，也像解谜一样困难。像举重一样困难，是因为说服别人拿出大笔钱本质上就很难。这个问题无法简化；它本来就应该是困难的。但另一种困难在很大程度上是可以消除的。融资之所以看起来像谜题，是因为对大多数创始人来说这是一个陌生的世界，我希望通过提供一张地图来帮助解决这个问题。

To founders, the behavior of investors is often opaque — partly because their motivations are obscure, but partly because they deliberately mislead you. And the misleading ways of investors combine horribly with the wishful thinking of inexperienced founders. At YC we’re always warning founders about this danger, and investors are probably more circumspect with YC startups than with other companies they talk to, and even so we witness a constant series of explosions as these two volatile components combine. [1]

对创始人来说，投资者的行为常常是不透明的——部分原因是他们的动机不明，部分原因是他们故意误导你。而投资者的误导方式与缺乏经验的创始人的一厢情愿结合得极其糟糕。在 YC，我们总是警告创始人注意这种危险，投资者对 YC 初创公司可能比对其他公司更加谨慎，即便如此，我们仍然目睹了这两种不稳定成分结合后不断发生的爆炸。【1】

If you’re an inexperienced founder, the only way to survive is by imposing external constraints on yourself. You can’t trust your intuitions. I’m going to give you a set of rules here that will get you through this process if anything will. At certain moments you’ll be tempted to ignore them. So rule number zero is: these rules exist for a reason. You wouldn’t need a rule to keep you going in one direction if there weren’t powerful forces pushing you in another.

如果你是一位缺乏经验的创始人，唯一的生存之道就是给自己施加外部约束。你不能相信自己的直觉。我将在这里给你一套规则，如果有什么能帮你度过这个过程，那就是它们了。在某些时刻，你会忍不住忽视它们。所以第零条规则是：这些规则的存在是有原因的。如果没有强大的力量把你推向另一个方向，你就不需要一条规则来让你朝一个方向前进。

The ultimate source of the forces acting on you are the forces acting on investors. Investors are pinched between two kinds of fear: fear of investing in startups that fizzle, and fear of missing out on startups that take off. The cause of all this fear is the very thing that makes startups such attractive investments: the successful ones grow very fast. But that fast growth means investors can’t wait around. If you wait till a startup is obviously a success, it’s too late. To get the really high returns, you have to invest in startups when it’s still unclear how they’ll do. But that in turn makes investors nervous they’re about to invest in a flop. As indeed they often are. What investors would like to do, if they could, is wait. When a startup is only a few months old, every week that passes gives you significantly more information about them. But if you wait too long, other investors might take the deal away from you. And of course the other investors are all subject to the same forces. So what tends to happen is that they all wait as long as they can, then when some act the rest have to.

作用于你的力量的最终来源是作用于投资者的力量。投资者被夹在两种恐惧之间：害怕投资那些失败的初创公司，以及害怕错过那些腾飞的初创公司。所有这些恐惧的根源正是使初创公司成为如此有吸引力的投资的原因：成功的那些增长非常快。但这种快速增长意味着投资者不能坐等。如果你等到一家初创公司明显成功，那就太晚了。要获得真正的高回报，你必须在初创公司前景还不明朗时投资。但这反过来又让投资者紧张，担心自己即将投资一个失败的项目——而事实上他们经常如此。投资者想做的是，如果可能的话，等待。当一家初创公司只有几个月大时，每过一周你都会获得关于它的显著更多信息。但如果你等得太久，其他投资者可能会抢走这笔交易。当然，其他投资者也受制于同样的力量。所以往往发生的情况是，他们都尽可能长时间地等待，然后当一些人行动时，其余的人也不得不动。

Don’t raise money unless you want it and it wants you.

除非你想要钱并且钱也想要你，否则不要融资。

Such a high proportion of successful startups raise money that it might seem fundraising is one of the defining qualities of a startup. Actually it isn’t. Rapid growth is what makes a company a startup. Most companies in a position to grow rapidly find that (a) taking outside money helps them grow faster, and (b) their growth potential makes it easy to attract such money. It’s so common for both (a) and (b) to be true of a successful startup that practically all do raise outside money. But there may be cases where a startup either wouldn’t want to grow faster, or outside money wouldn’t help them to, and if you’re one of them, don’t raise money. The other time not to raise money is when you won’t be able to. If you try to raise money before you can convince investors, you’ll not only waste your time, but also burn your reputation with those investors.

成功的初创公司中有如此高的比例都融资了，以至于融资可能看起来像是初创公司的定义性特征之一。实际上并非如此。快速增长才是一家公司成为初创公司的原因。大多数处于快速增长位置的公司会发现：（a）外部资金帮助他们增长更快，（b）他们的增长潜力使吸引资金变得容易。对于一家成功的初创公司来说，（a）和（b）同时成立是如此普遍，以至于几乎所有成功的初创公司都会融资。但也可能存在这样的情况：一家初创公司要么不想增长更快，要么外部资金对他们没有帮助，如果你是其中之一，就不要融资。另一个不该融资的时候是当你无法融资的时候。如果你在能够说服投资者之前就尝试融资，你不仅会浪费自己的时间，还会在这些投资者那里消耗你的声誉。

Be in fundraising mode or not.

要么处于融资模式，要么不。

One of the things that surprises founders most about fundraising is how distracting it is. When you start fundraising, everything else grinds to a halt. The problem is not the time fundraising consumes but that it becomes the top idea in your mind. A startup can’t endure that level of distraction for long. An early stage startup grows mostly because the founders make it grow, and if the founders look away, growth usually drops sharply. Because fundraising is so distracting, a startup should either be in fundraising mode or not. And when you do decide to raise money, you should focus your whole attention on it so you can get it done quickly and get back to work. [2]

让创始人最感到惊讶的事情之一是融资有多分散注意力。当你开始融资时，其他一切都会停滞。问题不在于融资消耗的时间，而在于它成为你脑海中的最重要的想法。一家初创公司无法长期承受这种程度的分散注意力。早期初创公司的增长主要是因为创始人在推动它增长，如果创始人转移视线，增长通常会急剧下降。因为融资如此分散注意力，初创公司要么处于融资模式，要么不。当你决定融资时，你应该把全部注意力集中在上面，以便快速完成并回到工作中。【2】

You can take money from investors when you’re not in fundraising mode. You just can’t expend any attention on it. There are two things that take attention: convincing investors, and negotiating with them. So when you’re not in fundraising mode, you should take money from investors only if they require no convincing, and are willing to invest on terms you’ll take without negotiation. For example, if a reputable investor is willing to invest on a convertible note, using standard paperwork, that is either uncapped or capped at a good valuation, you can take that without having to think. [3] The terms will be whatever they turn out to be in your next equity round. And “no convincing” means just that: zero time spent meeting with investors or preparing materials for them. If an investor says they’re ready to invest, but they need you to come in for one meeting to meet some of the partners, tell them no, if you’re not in fundraising mode, because that’s fundraising. [4] Tell them politely; tell them you’re focusing on the company right now, and that you’ll get back to them when you’re fundraising; but do not get sucked down the slippery slope.

当你不在融资模式时，你也可以从投资者那里拿钱。只是你不能在上面花费任何注意力。有两件事会消耗注意力：说服投资者和与他们谈判。所以当你不在融资模式时，你应该只在投资者不需要说服、并且愿意以你不用谈判就能接受的条款投资的情况下，才接受他们的钱。例如，如果一位有声望的投资者愿意以可转换债券的形式投资，使用标准文件，无论是无上限还是有良好估值上限的，你都可以不加思考地接受。【3】条款将取决于你下一轮股权融资的结果。而“不需要说服”就是指：零时间花在与投资者会面或为他们准备材料上。如果一位投资者说他们准备好投资了，但需要你来开一次会见见一些合伙人，如果你不在融资模式，就告诉他们不，因为那就是融资。【4】礼貌地告诉他们；告诉他们你现在专注于公司，等你要融资时会再联系他们；但不要被拉下那个滑坡。

Investors will try to lure you into fundraising when you’re not. It’s great for them if they can, because they can thereby get a shot at you before everyone else. They’ll send you emails saying they want to meet to learn more about you. If you get cold-emailed by an associate at a VC firm, you shouldn’t meet even if you are in fundraising mode. Deals don’t happen that way. [5] But even if you get an email from a partner you should try to delay meeting till you’re in fundraising mode. They may say they just want to meet and chat, but investors never just want to meet and chat. What if they like you? What if they start to talk about giving you money? Will you be able to resist having that conversation? Unless you’re experienced enough at fundraising to have a casual conversation with investors that stays casual, it’s safer to tell them that you’d be happy to later, when you’re fundraising, but that right now you need to focus on the company. [6]

当你不在融资模式时，投资者会试图引诱你进入融资模式。如果他们能做到，这对他们来说很有利，因为他们可以因此比别人更早地接触到你们。他们会给你发邮件说想见面了解你更多。如果你收到一家风投公司合伙人的冷邮件，即使你在融资模式，你也不应该见面。交易不会以那种方式发生。【5】但即使你收到一位合伙人的邮件，你也应该尽量把会面推迟到你进入融资模式之后。他们可能会说只是想见面聊聊，但投资者从来不只是想见面聊聊。如果他们喜欢你呢？如果他们开始谈论给你钱呢？你能抵制住进行那种对话的诱惑吗？除非你在融资方面经验丰富，能够与投资者进行保持随意的对话，否则更安全的做法是告诉他们你很乐意以后在融资时再谈，但现在你需要专注于公司。【6】

Companies that are successful at raising money in phase 2 sometimes tack on a few investors after leaving fundraising mode. This is fine; if fundraising went well, you’ll be able to do it without spending time convincing them or negotiating about terms.

在第二阶段成功融资的公司有时会在离开融资模式后增加几个投资者。这没问题；如果融资进展顺利，你将能够在不需要花时间说服他们或谈判条款的情况下做到这一点。

Get introductions to investors.

获得投资者的引荐。

Before you can talk to investors, you have to be introduced to them. If you’re presenting at a Demo Day, you’ll be introduced to a whole bunch simultaneously. But even if you are, you should supplement these with intros you collect yourself. Do you have to be introduced? In phase 2, yes. Some investors will let you email them a business plan, but you can tell from the way their sites are organized that they don’t really want startups to approach them directly. Intros vary greatly in effectiveness. The best type of intro is from a well-known investor who has just invested in you. So when you get an investor to commit, ask them to introduce you to other investors they respect. [7] The next best type of intro is from a founder of a company they’ve funded. You can also get intros from other people in the startup community, like lawyers and reporters. There are now sites like AngelList, FundersClub, and WeFunder that can introduce you to investors. We recommend startups treat them as auxiliary sources of money. Raise money first from leads you get yourself. Those will on average be better investors. Plus you’ll have an easier time raising money on these sites once you can say you’ve already raised some from well-known investors.

在你可以与投资者交谈之前，你必须被引荐给他们。如果你在 Demo Day 上展示，你会同时被引荐给一大群人。但即使如此，你也应该用自己收集的引荐来补充。你必须被引荐吗？在第二阶段，是的。有些投资者会允许你给他们发商业计划书，但你可以从他们网站的布局方式看出，他们并不真的希望初创公司直接联系他们。引荐的效果差异很大。最好的一类引荐来自刚刚投资了你的知名投资者。所以当你让一位投资者承诺投资时，请他们把你介绍给他们尊重的其他投资者。【7】次好的引荐来自他们投资过的公司的创始人。你也可以从初创社区的其他人士那里获得引荐，比如律师和记者。现在有像 AngelList、FundersClub 和 WeFunder 这样的网站可以把你介绍给投资者。我们建议初创公司把它们当作辅助资金来源。先从你自己获得的线索那里融资。那些平均而言会是更好的投资者。此外，一旦你能说你已经从知名投资者那里融到了一些钱，你在这些网站上融资就会更容易。

Hear no till you hear yes.

在听到“是”之前都当作“不”。

Treat investors as saying no till they unequivocally say yes, in the form of a definite offer with no contingencies. I mentioned earlier that investors prefer to wait if they can. What’s particularly dangerous for founders is the way they wait. Essentially, they lead you on. They seem like they’re about to invest right up till the moment they say no. If they even say no. Some of the worse ones never actually do say no; they just stop replying to your emails. They hope that way to get a free option on investing. If they decide later that they want to invest — usually because they’ve heard you’re a hot deal — they can pretend they just got distracted and then restart the conversation as if they’d been about to. [8] That’s not the worst thing investors will do. Some will use language that makes it sound as if they’re committing, but which doesn’t actually commit them. And wishful thinking founders are happy to meet them half way. [9] Fortunately, the next rule is a tactic for neutralizing this behavior. But to work it depends on you not being tricked by the no that sounds like yes. It’s so common for founders to be misled/mistaken about this that we designed a protocol to fix the problem. If you believe an investor has committed, get them to confirm it. If you and they have different views of reality, whether the source of the discrepancy is their sketchiness or your wishful thinking, the prospect of confirming a commitment in writing will flush it out. And till they confirm, regard them as saying no.

把投资者当作一直在说“不”，直到他们明确地说“是”——以一份确定的、没有附带条件的要约形式。我之前提到过，投资者如果可能的话倾向于等待。对创始人来说特别危险的是他们等待的方式。本质上，他们是在吊着你。他们看起来好像马上就要投资了，直到说“不”的那一刻。如果他们甚至说了“不”的话。一些更差劲的投资者实际上从不说“不”；他们只是停止回复你的邮件。他们希望通过这种方式获得一个免费的投资期权。如果他们后来决定想投资——通常是因为他们听说你是一笔热门的交易——他们可以假装只是分心了，然后重新开始对话，就好像他们本来就要投资一样。【8】那还不是投资者会做的最糟糕的事。有些人会使用听起来像在承诺的语言，但实际上并没有承诺他们什么。而一厢情愿的创始人很乐意配合他们。【9】幸运的是，下一条规则是中和这种行为的策略。但它的有效性取决于你不上当受骗，不被那种听起来像“是”的“不”所迷惑。创始人在这个问题上被误导/犯错是如此普遍，以至于我们设计了一个协议来解决这个问题。如果你认为一位投资者已经承诺了，让他们确认。如果你和他们对现实有不同的看法，无论分歧的来源是他们的不靠谱还是你的一厢情愿，以书面形式确认承诺的前景都会让真相浮出水面。在他们确认之前，都把他们当作在说“不”。

Do breadth-first search weighted by expected value.

按期望值加权进行广度优先搜索。

When you talk to investors your m.o. should be breadth-first search, weighted by expected value. You should always talk to investors in parallel rather than serially. You can’t afford the time it takes to talk to investors serially, plus if you only talk to one investor at a time, they don’t have the pressure of other investors to make them act. But you shouldn’t pay the same attention to every investor, because some are more promising prospects than others. The optimal solution is to talk to all potential investors in parallel, but give higher priority to the more promising ones. [10] Expected value = how likely an investor is to say yes, multiplied by how good it would be if they did. So for example, an eminent investor who would invest a lot, but will be hard to convince, might have the same expected value as an obscure angel who won’t invest much, but will be easy to convince. Whereas an obscure angel who will only invest a small amount, and yet needs to meet multiple times before making up his mind, has very low expected value. Meet such investors last, if at all. [11] Doing breadth-first search weighted by expected value will save you from investors who never explicitly say no but merely drift away, because you’ll drift away from them at the same rate. It protects you from investors who flake in much the same way that a distributed algorithm protects you from processors that fail. If some investor isn’t returning your emails, or wants to have lots of meetings but isn’t progressing toward making you an offer, you automatically focus less on them. But you have to be disciplined about assigning probabilities. You can’t let how much you want an investor influence your estimate of how much they want you.

当你与投资者交谈时，你的做法应该是按期望值加权的广度优先搜索。你应该总是并行地而非串行地与投资者交谈。你承担不起串行交谈所需的时间，而且如果你一次只和一位投资者谈，他们就没有其他投资者的压力来促使他们行动。但你不应该对每个投资者都给予同样的关注，因为有些比其他的更有前景。最优解是与所有潜在投资者并行交谈，但给更有前景的那些更高的优先级。【10】期望值 = 投资者说“是”的可能性乘以如果他们说了会有多好。所以，例如，一位会投资很多但很难说服的知名投资者，可能与一位不太知名、投资不多但容易说服的天使具有相同的期望值。而一位只会投资少量金额、却需要多次会面才能下定决心的不太知名的天使，期望值非常低。这种投资者最后再见，如果还值得见的话。【11】按期望值加权进行广度优先搜索将使你免受那些从不明确说“不”而只是逐渐疏远的投资者的伤害，因为你会以同样的速度逐渐疏远他们。它保护你免受那些不靠谱的投资者，就像分布式算法保护你免受故障处理器的影响一样。如果某个投资者不回你的邮件，或者想要开很多会但没有朝着给你发要约的方向进展，你自然会减少对他们的关注。但你必须自律地分配概率。你不能让你对一位投资者的渴望影响你对他们对你的渴望程度的估计。

Know where you stand.

知道你的位置。

How do you judge how well you’re doing with an investor, when investors habitually seem more positive than they are? By looking at their actions rather than their words. Every investor has some track they need to move along from the first conversation to wiring the money, and you should always know what that track consists of, where you are on it, and how fast you’re moving forward. Never leave a meeting with an investor without asking what happens next. What more do they need in order to decide? Do they need another meeting with you? To talk about what? And how soon? Do they need to do something internally, like talk to their partners, or investigate some issue? How long do they expect it to take? Don’t be too pushy, but know where you stand. If investors are vague or resist answering such questions, assume the worst; investors who are seriously interested in you will usually be happy to talk about what has to happen between now and wiring the money, because they’re already running through that in their heads. [12] If you’re experienced at negotiations, you already know how to ask such questions. [13] If you’re not, there’s a trick you can use in this situation. Investors know you’re inexperienced at raising money. Inexperience there doesn’t make you unattractive. Being a noob at technology would, if you’re starting a technology startup, but not being a noob at fundraising. Larry and Sergey were noobs at fundraising. So you can just confess that you’re inexperienced at this and ask how their process works and where you are in it. [14]

当投资者习惯于表现得比实际情况更积极时，你如何判断你和投资者的关系进展如何？通过看他们的行动而不是言语。每个投资者都有一条从第一次对话到打款需要走过的轨道，你应该始终知道这条轨道由什么组成，你在上面处于什么位置，以及你前进的速度有多快。永远不要在离开投资者会议时不问接下来会发生什么。他们还需要什么才能做决定？他们需要再和你开一次会吗？谈什么？多快？他们需要做一些内部的事情吗，比如和合伙人谈谈，或调查某个问题？他们预计需要多长时间？不要太咄咄逼人，但要知道你的位置。如果投资者含糊其辞或拒绝回答这些问题，假设最坏的情况；对你真正感兴趣的投资者通常乐于谈论从现在到打款之间需要发生什么，因为他们已经在脑子里过了一遍了。【12】如果你在谈判方面有经验，你已经知道如何问这类问题。【13】如果你没有，这种情况有一个技巧可以用。投资者知道你在融资方面缺乏经验。这方面的不成熟并不会让你失去吸引力。如果你在创办一家科技初创公司，在技术方面是新手会有问题，但在融资方面是新手则不会。Larry 和 Sergey 在融资方面也是新手。所以你可以直接承认你在这方面没有经验，并问他们的流程是如何运作的，以及你处于什么位置。【14】

Get the first commitment.

获得第一个承诺。

The biggest factor in most investors’ opinions of you is the opinion of other investors. Once you start getting investors to commit, it becomes increasingly easy to get more to. But the other side of this coin is that it’s often hard to get the first commitment. Getting the first substantial offer can be half the total difficulty of fundraising. What counts as a substantial offer depends on who it’s from and how much it is. Money from friends and family doesn’t usually count, no matter how much. But if you get $50k from a well known VC firm or angel investor, that will usually be enough to set things rolling. [15]

大多数投资者对你的看法中最大的因素，是其他投资者的看法。一旦你开始让投资者承诺，让更多的人承诺就变得越来越容易。但硬币的另一面是，获得第一个承诺往往很难。获得第一份实质性要约可能是融资总难度的一半。什么算作实质性要约取决于来自谁以及金额多少。来自家人和朋友的钱通常不算，无论多少。但如果你从一家知名风投公司或天使投资人那里获得 5 万美元，那通常就足以让事情运转起来了。【15】

Close committed money.

锁定已承诺的资金。

It’s not a deal till the money’s in the bank. I often hear inexperienced founders say things like “We’ve raised $800,000,” only to discover that zero of it is in the bank so far. Remember the twin fears that torment investors? The fear of missing out that makes them jump early, and the fear of jumping onto a turd that results? This is a market where people are exceptionally prone to buyer’s remorse. And it’s also one that furnishes them plenty of excuses to gratify it. The public markets snap startup investing around like a whip. If the Chinese economy blows up tomorrow, all bets are off. But there are lots of surprises for individual startups too, and they tend to be concentrated around fundraising. Tomorrow a big competitor could appear, or you could get C&Ded, or your cofounder could quit. [16] Even a day’s delay can bring news that causes an investor to change their mind. So when someone commits, get the money. Knowing where you stand doesn’t end when they say they’ll invest. After they say yes, know what the timetable is for getting the money, and then babysit that process till it happens. Institutional investors have people in charge of wiring money, but you may have to hunt angels down in person to collect a check. Inexperienced investors are the ones most likely to get buyer’s remorse. Established ones have learned to treat saying yes as like diving off a diving board, and they also have more brand to preserve. But I’ve heard of cases of even top-tier VC firms welching on deals.

钱到账之前都不算交易。我经常听到缺乏经验的创始人说“我们已经融了 80 万美元”，结果发现到目前为止一分钱都没到账。还记得折磨投资者的那两种恐惧吗？害怕错过让他们早早行动，以及害怕踩到狗屎的结果？这是一个人们特别容易产生“买家后悔”的市场。而且这也是一个为他们提供了大量借口来满足这种后悔的市场。公开市场像鞭子一样抽打着初创投资。如果明天中国经济崩盘，所有赌注都作废。但对单个初创公司来说也有很多意外，而且它们往往集中在融资期间。明天可能会出现一个大竞争对手，或者你可能会收到停止侵权函，或者你的联合创始人可能会退出。【16】即使是一天的延迟也可能带来让投资者改变主意的消息。所以当有人承诺时，拿到钱。知道你的位置并不会在他们说会投资时就结束。在他们说“是”之后，要知道拿到钱的时间表，然后监督这个过程直到完成。机构投资者有负责打款的人，但你可能需要亲自去追天使投资人拿支票。缺乏经验的投资者最有可能产生“买家后悔”。成熟的投资者已经学会了把说“是”当作从跳板上跳下去，而且他们也有更多的品牌需要维护。但我也听说过即使是顶级风投公司也有食言的情况。

Avoid investors who don’t “lead.”

避开那些不“领投”的投资者。

Since getting the first offer is most of the difficulty of fundraising, that should be part of your calculation of expected value when you start. You have to estimate not just the probability that an investor will say yes, but the probability that they’d be the first to say yes, and the latter is not simply a constant fraction of the former. Some investors are known for deciding quickly, and those are extra valuable early on. Conversely, an investor who will only invest once other investors have is worthless initially. And while most investors are influenced by how interested other investors are in you, there are some who have an explicit policy of only investing after other investors have. You can recognize this contemptible subspecies of investor because they often talk about “leads.” They say that they don’t lead, or that they’ll invest once you have a lead. Sometimes they even claim to be willing to lead themselves, by which they mean they won’t invest till you get $x from other investors. (It’s great if by “lead” they mean they’ll invest unilaterally, and in addition will help you raise more. What’s lame is when they use the term to mean they won’t invest unless you can raise more elsewhere.) [17] Where does this term “lead” come from? Up till a few years ago, startups raising money in phase 2 would usually raise equity rounds in which several investors invested at the same time using the same paperwork. You’d negotiate the terms with one “lead” investor, and then all the others would sign the same documents and all the money change hands at the closing. Series A rounds still work that way, but things now work differently for most fundraising prior to the series A. Now there are rarely actual rounds before the A round, or leads for them. Now startups simply raise money from investors one at a time till they feel they have enough. Since there are no longer leads, why do investors use that term? Because it’s a more legitimate-sounding way of saying what they really mean. All they really mean is that their interest in you is a function of other investors’ interest in you. I.e. the spectral signature of all mediocre investors. But when phrased in terms of leads, it sounds like there is something structural and therefore legitimate about their behavior. When an investor tells you “I want to invest in you, but I don’t lead,” translate that in your mind to “No, except yes if you turn out to be a hot deal.” And since that’s the default opinion of any investor about any startup, they’ve essentially just told you nothing. When you first start fundraising, the expected value of an investor who won’t “lead” is zero, so talk to such investors last if at all.

既然获得第一份要约是融资的大部分难度，那在你开始时就应该是你计算期望值的一部分。你不仅需要估计投资者说“是”的概率，还需要估计他们成为第一个说“是”的概率，而后者不仅仅是前者的一个固定分数。有些投资者以决策迅速而闻名，这些人在早期特别有价值。相反，一个只有在其他投资者投资之后才会投资的投资者，在初期毫无价值。虽然大多数投资者会受到其他投资者对你的兴趣程度的影响，但有些人的明确政策是只有在其他投资者投资之后才投资。你可以识别这种可鄙的投资者亚种，因为他们经常谈论“领投”。他们说他们不领投，或者他们会在你有领投人之后才投资。有时他们甚至声称愿意自己领投，但他们的意思其实是他们不会投资，直到你从其他投资者那里拿到 X 美元。（如果“领投”的意思是他们会单方面投资，并且还会帮你筹集更多资金，那就很好。差劲的是他们用这个词来表示除非你从别处筹到更多钱，否则他们不会投资。）【17】“领投”这个词从何而来？直到几年前，在第二阶段融资的初创公司通常进行股权融资，多个投资者同时使用相同的文件投资。你会与一个“领投”投资者谈判条款，然后所有其他投资者签署相同的文件，所有资金在交割时易手。A 轮融资仍然以那种方式进行，但现在大多数 A 轮之前的融资情况已经不同了。现在在 A 轮之前很少有真正的“轮次”，也没有“领投人”。现在初创公司只是从投资者那里一个接一个地融资，直到他们觉得够了。既然不再有领投人了，为什么投资者还用这个词？因为这是说出他们真正意思的听起来更合理的说法。他们真正的意思是他们对你的兴趣是其他投资者对你的兴趣的函数。即所有平庸投资者的光谱特征。但当用“领投”的措辞来表达时，听起来好像他们的行为有某种结构性的、因此是合理的依据。当一位投资者告诉你“我想投资你，但我不领投”时，在心里把它翻译成“不，除非你变成一笔热门的交易，那就是是”。因为那是任何投资者对任何初创公司的默认看法，所以他们基本上什么也没告诉你。当你刚开始融资时，一个不愿意“领投”的投资者期望值为零，所以这种投资者最后再见，如果还值得见的话。

Have multiple plans.

拥有多个计划。

Many investors will ask how much you’re planning to raise. This question makes founders feel they should be planning to raise a specific amount. But in fact you shouldn’t. It’s a mistake to have fixed plans in an undertaking as unpredictable as fundraising. So why do investors ask how much you plan to raise? For much the same reasons a salesperson in a store will ask “How much were you planning to spend?” if you walk in looking for a gift for a friend. You probably didn’t have a precise amount in mind; you just want to find something good, and if it’s inexpensive, so much the better. The salesperson asks you this not because you’re supposed to have a plan to spend a specific amount, but so they can show you only things that cost the most you’ll pay. Similarly, when investors ask how much you plan to raise, it’s not because you’re supposed to have a plan. It’s to see whether you’d be a suitable recipient for the size of investment they like to make, and also to judge your ambition, reasonableness, and how far you are along with fundraising. If you’re a wizard at fundraising, you can say “We plan to raise a $7 million series A round, and we’ll be accepting termsheets next tuesday.” I’ve known a handful of founders who could pull that off without having VCs laugh in their faces. But if you’re in the inexperienced but earnest majority, the solution is analogous to the solution I recommend for pitching your startup: do the right thing and then just tell investors what you’re doing. And the right strategy, in fundraising, is to have multiple plans depending on how much you can raise. Ideally you should be able to tell investors something like: we can make it to profitability without raising any more money, but if we raise a few hundred thousand we can hire one or two smart friends, and if we raise a couple million, we can hire a whole engineering team, etc. Different plans match different investors. If you’re talking to a VC firm that only does series A rounds (though there are few of those left), it would be a waste of time talking about any but your most expensive plan. Whereas if you’re talking to an angel who invests $20k at a time and you haven’t raised any money yet, you probably want to focus on your least expensive plan. If you’re so fortunate as to have to think about the upper limit on what you should raise, a good rule of thumb is to multiply the number of people you want to hire times $15k times 18 months. In most startups, nearly all the costs are a function of the number of people, and $15k per month is the conventional total cost (including benefits and even office space) per person. $15k per month is high, so don’t actually spend that much. But it’s ok to use a high estimate when fundraising to add a margin for error. If you have additional expenses, like manufacturing, add in those at the end. Assuming you have none and you think you might hire 20 people, the most you’d want to raise is 20 x $15k x 18 = $5.4 million. [18]

许多投资者会问你计划融多少钱。这个问题让创始人觉得他们应该计划融一个具体的金额。但实际上你不应该。在一项像融资这样不可预测的事业中，有固定的计划是错误的。那么投资者为什么要问你计划融多少钱呢？原因和商店里的销售人员在你走进来为朋友挑选礼物时会问“你打算花多少钱？”大致相同。你心里可能没有精确的金额；你只是想找到好东西，如果它便宜，那就更好了。销售人员问你这个问题不是因为你应该有一个花特定金额的计划，而是为了他们可以只给你看你愿意支付的最高价格的东西。同样，当投资者问你计划融多少钱时，不是因为你应该有一个计划。而是为了看看你是否适合他们喜欢做的投资规模，也是为了判断你的雄心、合理性以及你在融资方面的进展程度。如果你是融资方面的奇才，你可以说“我们计划融 700 万美元的 A 轮，我们下周二接受投资意向书”。我认识少数能这样做到而不会被风投当面嘲笑的创始人。但如果你是缺乏经验但真诚的大多数，解决方案类似于我推荐的推销你的初创公司的方法：做正确的事情，然后直接告诉投资者你在做什么。而在融资方面，正确的策略是根据你能融多少钱制定多个计划。理想情况下，你应该能够告诉投资者类似这样的话：我们不融更多钱也能实现盈利，但如果我们融几十万，我们可以雇一两个聪明的朋友，如果我们融几百万，我们可以雇一整个工程团队，等等。不同的计划匹配不同的投资者。如果你在和一个只做 A 轮的风投公司谈话（虽然这样的公司已经不多了），谈论除了你最昂贵的计划之外的任何计划都是浪费时间。而如果你在和一个每次投资 2 万美元的天使谈话，而且你还没有融到任何钱，你可能想专注于你最便宜的计划。如果你幸运到需要考虑应该融资的上限，一个好的经验法则是将你想雇佣的人数乘以 1.5 万美元再乘以 18 个月。在大多数初创公司中，几乎所有的成本都是人数的函数，每人每月 1.5 万美元是传统的总成本（包括福利甚至办公空间）。每月 1.5 万美元很高，所以不要真的花那么多。但在融资时使用较高的估计来增加误差余量是可以的。如果你有额外费用，比如制造费用，最后再加进去。假设你没有额外费用，并且你认为你可能雇佣 20 人，那么你最想融资的上限是 20 x 1.5 万 x 18 = 540 万美元。【18】

Underestimate how much you want.

低估你想要多少。

Though you can focus on different plans when talking to different types of investors, you should on the whole err on the side of underestimating the amount you hope to raise. For example, if you’d like to raise $500k, it’s better to say initially that you’re trying to raise $250k. Then when you reach $150k you’re more than half done. That sends two useful signals to investors: that you’re doing well, and that they have to decide quickly because you’re running out of room. Whereas if you’d said you were raising $500k, you’d be less than a third done at $150k. If fundraising stalled there for an appreciable time, you’d start to read as a failure. Saying initially that you’re raising $250k doesn’t limit you to raising that much. When you reach your initial target and you still have investor interest, you can just decide to raise more. Startups do that all the time. In fact, most startups that are very successful at fundraising end up raising more than they originally intended. I’m not saying you should lie, but that you should lower your expectations initially. There is almost no downside in starting with a low number. It not only won’t cap the amount you raise, but will on the whole tend to increase it. A good metaphor here is angle of attack. If you try to fly at too steep an angle of attack, you just stall. If you say right out of the gate that you want to raise a $5 million series A round, unless you’re in a very strong position, you not only won’t get that but won’t get anything. Better to start at a low angle of attack, build up speed, and then gradually increase the angle if you want.

虽然你在与不同类型的投资者交谈时可以专注于不同的计划，但总的来说，你应该倾向于低估你希望融资的金额。例如，如果你想融 50 万美元，最好一开始说你在努力融 25 万美元。然后当你达到 15 万美元时，你已经完成了一半以上。这向投资者发出了两个有用的信号：你做得很好，而且他们必须快速决定，因为你的额度快满了。而如果你说你融 50 万美元，在 15 万美元时你还不到三分之一。如果融资在那里停滞了相当长的时间，你就会被视为失败。一开始说你在融 25 万美元并不会限制你只融那么多。当你达到初始目标并且仍然有投资者感兴趣时，你可以决定融更多。初创公司经常这样做。事实上，大多数在融资方面非常成功的初创公司最终融到的钱都比最初打算的多。我不是说你应该撒谎，而是说你应该在最初降低期望。从一个低数字开始几乎没有坏处。它不仅不会限制你融到的金额，而且总体上往往会增加它。这里有一个好的比喻是攻角。如果你试图以太陡的攻角飞行，你就会失速。如果你一上来就说你想融 500 万美元的 A 轮，除非你处于非常强势的位置，否则你不仅拿不到那个，而且什么都拿不到。最好从低攻角开始，积累速度，然后如果你愿意再逐渐增加角度。

Be profitable if you can.

如果可能的话，实现盈利。

You will be in a much stronger position if your collection of plans includes one for raising zero dollars — i.e. if you can make it to profitability without raising any additional money. Ideally you want to be able to say to investors “We’ll succeed no matter what, but raising money will help us do it faster.” There are many analogies between fundraising and dating, and this is one of the strongest. No one wants you if you seem desperate. And the best way not to seem desperate is not to be desperate. That’s one reason we urge startups during YC to keep expenses low and to try to make it to ramen profitability before Demo Day. Though it sounds slightly paradoxical, if you want to raise money, the best thing you can do is get yourself to the point where you don’t need to. There are almost two distinct modes of fundraising: one in which founders who need money knock on doors seeking it, knowing that otherwise the company will die or at the very least people will have to be fired, and one in which founders who don’t need money take some to grow faster than they could merely on their own revenues. To emphasize the distinction I’m going to name them: type A fundraising is when you don’t need money, and type B fundraising is when you do. Inexperienced founders read about famous startups doing what was type A fundraising, and decide they should raise money too, since that seems to be how startups work. Except when they raise money they don’t have a clear path to profitability and are thus doing type B fundraising. And they are then surprised how difficult and unpleasant it is. Of course not all startups can make it to ramen profitability in a few months. And some that don’t still manage to have the upper hand over investors, if they have some other advantage like extraordinary growth numbers or exceptionally formidable founders. But as time passes it gets increasingly difficult to fundraise from a position of strength without being profitable. [19]

如果你的计划组合中包括一个融零美元的计划——也就是说，如果你可以在不融更多钱的情况下实现盈利——你将处于更强大的位置。理想情况下，你希望能够对投资者说：“无论如何我们都会成功，但融资会帮助我们更快地做到。”融资和约会之间有很多类比，这是最强有力的一个。如果你看起来很绝望，没人想要你。而看起来不绝望的最好方式就是不绝望。这就是为什么我们在 YC 期间敦促初创公司保持低开支，并尝试在 Demo Day 之前达到拉面盈利能力。虽然听起来有点矛盾，但如果你想融资，你能做的最好的事情就是让自己达到不需要钱的地步。几乎有两种截然不同的融资模式：一种是需要钱的创始人敲门寻求资金，知道否则公司会死掉或者至少人们会被解雇；另一种是不需要钱的创始人接受一些资金来比仅靠自己的收入增长得更快。为了强调这种区别，我给它们命名：A 类融资是当你不需要钱的时候，B 类融资是当你需要钱的时候。缺乏经验的创始人读到著名初创公司做的是 A 类融资，就决定他们也应该融资，因为那似乎是初创公司的运作方式。但他们融资时并没有清晰的盈利路径，因此做的是 B 类融资。然后他们对融资有多困难和令人不快感到惊讶。当然，并非所有初创公司都能在几个月内达到拉面盈利。有些做不到的仍然能够在对投资者的博弈中占据上风，如果他们有其他优势，比如非凡的增长数字或特别强大的创始人。但随着时间的推移，在不盈利的情况下从强势地位融资变得越来越困难。【19】

Don’t optimize for valuation.

不要优化估值。

When you raise money, what should your valuation be? The most important thing to understand about valuation is that it’s not that important. Founders who raise money at high valuations tend to be unduly proud of it. Founders are often competitive people, and since valuation is usually the only visible number attached to a startup, they end up competing to raise money at the highest valuation. This is stupid, because fundraising is not the test that matters. The real test is revenue. Fundraising is just a means to that end. Being proud of how well you did at fundraising is like being proud of your college grades. Not only is fundraising not the test that matters, valuation is not even the thing to optimize about fundraising. The number one thing you want from phase 2 fundraising is to get the money you need, so you can get back to focusing on the real test, the success of your company. Number two is good investors. Valuation is at best third. The empirical evidence shows just how unimportant it is. Dropbox and Airbnb are the most successful companies we’ve funded so far, and they raised money after Y Combinator at premoney valuations of $4 million and $2.6 million respectively. Prices are so much higher now that if you can raise money at all you’ll probably raise it at higher valuations than Dropbox and Airbnb. So let that satisfy your competitiveness. You’re doing better than Dropbox and Airbnb! At a test that doesn’t matter. When you start fundraising, your initial valuation (or valuation cap) will be set by the deal you make with the first investor who commits. You can increase the price for later investors, if you get a lot of interest, but by default the valuation you got from the first investor becomes your asking price. So if you’re raising money from multiple investors, as most companies do in phase 2, you have to be careful to avoid raising the first from an over-eager investor at a price you won’t be able to sustain. You can of course lower your price if you need to (in which case you should give the same terms to investors who invested earlier at a higher price), but you may lose a bunch of leads in the process of realizing you need to do this. What you can do if you have eager first investors is raise money from them on an uncapped convertible note with an MFN clause. This is essentially a way of saying that the valuation cap of the note will be determined by the next investors you raise money from. It will be easier to raise money at a lower valuation. It shouldn’t be, but it is. Since phase 2 prices vary at most 10x and the big successes generate returns of at least 100x, investors should pick startups entirely based on their estimate of the probability that the company will be a big success and hardly at all on price. But although it’s a mistake for investors to care about price, a significant number do. A startup that investors seem to like but won’t invest in at a cap of $x will have an easier time at $x/2. [20]

当你融资时，你的估值应该是多少？关于估值，最重要的事情是理解它并没有那么重要。以高估值融资的创始人往往对此过分自豪。创始人通常是有竞争心的人，既然估值通常是附着在初创公司上的唯一可见数字，他们最终会竞争以最高估值融资。这是愚蠢的，因为融资并不是重要的考验。真正的考验是收入。融资只是达到那个目的的手段。为你融资做得有多好而自豪，就像为你的大学成绩自豪一样。不仅融资不是重要的考验，估值甚至不是融资中需要优化的东西。你在第二阶段融资中想要的第一件事是获得你需要的钱，这样你就可以回到关注真正的考验——你公司的成功。第二件事是好的投资者。估值最多排在第三位。经验证据表明它有多不重要。Dropbox 和 Airbnb 是我们迄今为止资助的最成功的公司，它们在 Y Combinator 之后融资时的融资前估值分别为 400 万美元和 260 万美元。现在的价格高得多，如果你能融资的话，你可能会以比 Dropbox 和 Airbnb 更高的估值融资。所以让那满足你的竞争心吧。你比 Dropbox 和 Airbnb 做得更好！在一个不重要的测试上。当你开始融资时，你的初始估值（或估值上限）将由你与第一个承诺的投资者达成的交易设定。如果你获得了很多兴趣，你可以提高后续投资者的价格，但默认情况下，你从第一个投资者那里获得的估值就是你的要价。所以如果你从多个投资者那里融资，正如大多数公司在第二阶段所做的那样，你必须小心避免以过高的价格从过于急切的投资者那里融第一笔钱，导致你无法维持。你当然可以在需要时降低价格（在这种情况下，你应该给以更高价格投资的早期投资者同样的条款），但你可能在意识到需要这样做时失去一堆线索。如果你有急切的早期投资者，你能做的是以无上限的可转换债券加上 MFN 条款从他们那里融资。这本质上是一种方式，说明债券的估值上限将由你接下来融资的投资者决定。以较低的估值融资会更容易。不应该这样，但事实就是如此。既然第二阶段的价格变化最多 10 倍，而大成功的回报至少 100 倍，投资者应该完全基于他们对公司会成为大成功的概率的估计来选择初创公司，而几乎不基于价格。但尽管投资者在意价格是错误的，还是有相当一部分人在意。一个投资者似乎喜欢但不会在 X 估值上限投资的初创公司，在 X/2 时会更容易。【20】

Yes/no before valuation.

先决定“是/否”，再谈估值。

Some investors want to know what your valuation is before they even talk to you about investing. If your valuation has already been set by a prior investment at a specific valuation or cap, you can tell them that number. But if it isn’t set because you haven’t closed anyone yet, and they try to push you to name a price, resist doing so. If this would be the first investor you’ve closed, then this could be the tipping point of fundraising. That means closing this investor is the first priority, and you need to get the conversation onto that instead of being dragged sideways into a discussion of price. Fortunately there is a way to avoid naming a price in this situation. And it is not just a negotiating trick; it’s how you (both) should be operating. Tell them that valuation is not the most important thing to you and that you haven’t thought much about it, that you are looking for investors you want to partner with and who want to partner with you, and that you should talk first about whether they want to invest at all. Then if they decide they do want to invest, you can figure out a price. But first things first. Since valuation isn’t that important and getting fundraising rolling is, we usually tell founders to give the first investor who commits as low a price as they need to. This is a safe technique so long as you combine it with the next one. [21]

有些投资者想在他们甚至和你谈投资之前就知道你的估值是多少。如果你的估值已经被之前的投资以特定的估值或上限设定了，你可以告诉他们那个数字。但如果还没有设定，因为你还没有锁定任何人，而他们试图迫使你报价，抵制这样做。如果这将是你锁定的第一个投资者，那么这可能是融资的转折点。这意味着锁定这个投资者是第一优先事项，你需要把对话引向那个方向，而不是被拖入关于价格的讨论。幸运的是，有一种方法可以避免在这种情况下报价。这不仅仅是一个谈判技巧；这是你们（双方）应该有的运作方式。告诉他们估值对你来说不是最重要的事情，你还没有想太多，你在寻找你希望与之合作的投资者，以及希望与你合作的投资者，你们应该先谈谈他们是否想投资。然后如果他们决定想投资，你们可以再确定价格。但先谈主要的事情。既然估值没有那么重要，而启动融资很重要，我们通常告诉创始人给第一个承诺的投资者尽可能低的价格，只要他们需要。这是一个安全的技术，只要你把它和下一条结合起来。【21】

Beware “valuation sensitive” investors.

警惕“估值敏感”的投资者。

Occasionally you’ll encounter investors who describe themselves as “valuation sensitive.” What this means in practice is that they are compulsive negotiators who will suck up a lot of your time trying to push your price down. You should therefore never approach such investors first. While you shouldn’t chase high valuations, you also don’t want your valuation to be set artificially low because the first investor who committed happened to be a compulsive negotiator. Some such investors have value, but the time to approach them is near the end of fundraising, when you’re in a position to say “this is the price everyone else has paid; take it or leave it” and not mind if they leave it. This way, you’ll not only get market price, but it will also take less time. Ideally you know which investors have a reputation for being “valuation sensitive” and can postpone dealing with them till last, but occasionally one you didn’t know about will pop up early on. The rule of doing breadth first search weighted by expected value already tells you what to do in this case: slow down your interactions with them. There are a handful of investors who will try to invest at a lower valuation even when your price has already been set. Lowering your price is a backup plan you resort to when you discover you’ve let the price get set too high to close all the money you need. So you’d only want to talk to this sort of investor if you were about to do that anyway. But since investor meetings have to be arranged at least a few days in advance and you can’t predict when you’ll need to resort to lowering your price, this means in practice that you should approach this type of investor last if at all. If you’re surprised by a lowball offer, treat it as a backup offer and delay responding to it. When someone makes an offer in good faith, you have a moral obligation to respond in a reasonable time. But lowballing you is a dick move that should be met with the corresponding countermove.

偶尔你会遇到自称“估值敏感”的投资者。这在实际中意味着他们是强迫性的谈判者，会消耗你大量时间试图压低你的价格。因此，你永远不应该首先接触这样的投资者。虽然你不应该追求高估值，但你也不希望你的估值因为第一个承诺的投资者恰好是一个强迫性谈判者而被人为地压低。这类投资者中有些是有价值的，但接触他们的时机是在融资接近尾声时，当你有能力说“这是其他人支付的价格；接受或不接受”并且不介意他们不接受时。这样，你不仅能获得市场价格，而且花费的时间也更少。理想情况下，你知道哪些投资者以“估值敏感”著称，可以把与他们打交道推迟到最后，但偶尔会有你不知道的早期出现。按期望值加权进行广度优先搜索的规则已经告诉你在这种情况下该怎么做：减慢与他们的互动。有少数投资者即使你的价格已经设定，也会试图以更低的估值投资。降低你的价格是当你发现价格设定得太高而无法完成你需要的融资时采用的备用计划。所以只有当你无论如何都要这样做时，你才想和这类投资者谈。但由于投资者会议必须至少提前几天安排，而且你无法预测何时需要降低价格，这实际上意味着你应该最后再接触这类投资者，如果还值得的话。如果你被一个低价报价惊讶到，把它当作一个备用报价，推迟回应。当有人真诚地提出要约时，你有道德义务在合理的时间内回应。但低价报价是一种混蛋行为，应该以相应的反制措施应对。

Accept offers greedily.

贪婪地接受要约。

I’m a little leery of using the term “greedily” when writing about fundraising lest non-programmers misunderstand me, but a greedy algorithm is simply one that doesn’t try to look into the future. A greedy algorithm takes the best of the options in front of it right now. And that is how startups should approach fundraising in phases 2 and later. Don’t try to look into the future because (a) the future is unpredictable, and indeed in this business you’re often being deliberately misled about it and (b) your first priority in fundraising should be to get it finished and get back to work anyway. If someone makes you an acceptable offer, take it. If you have multiple incompatible offers, take the best. Don’t reject an acceptable offer in the hope of getting a better one in the future.

我有点犹豫在写融资时使用“贪婪地”这个词，以免非程序员误解我，但贪婪算法只是一种不尝试展望未来的算法。贪婪算法接受当前摆在面前的最佳选项。这就是初创公司在第二阶段及以后应该采取的融资方式。不要尝试展望未来，因为（a）未来是不可预测的，而且在这个行业里，你经常被故意误导关于它的情况；（b）你在融资中的首要任务应该是尽快完成它并回到工作中。如果有人给你一个可接受的要约，接受它。如果你有多个不相容的要约，接受最好的。不要为了希望在未来获得更好的要约而拒绝一个可接受的要约。

These simple rules cover a wide variety of cases. If you’re raising money from many investors, roll them up as they say yes. As you start to feel you’ve raised enough, the threshold for acceptable will start to get higher. In practice offers exist for stretches of time, not points. So when you get an acceptable offer that would be incompatible with others (e.g. an offer to invest most of the money you need), you can tell the other investors you’re talking to that you have an offer good enough to accept, and give them a few days to make their own. This could lose you some that might have made an offer if they had more time. But by definition you don’t care; the initial offer was acceptable. Some investors will try to prevent others from having time to decide by giving you an “exploding” offer, meaning one that’s only valid for a few days. Offers from the very best investors explode less frequently and less rapidly — Fred Wilson never gives exploding offers, for example — because they’re confident you’ll pick them. But lower-tier investors sometimes give offers with very short fuses, because they believe no one who had other options would choose them. A deadline of three working days is acceptable. You shouldn’t need more than that if you’ve been talking to investors in parallel. But a deadline any shorter is a sign you’re dealing with a sketchy investor. You can usually call their bluff, and you may need to. [22]

这些简单的规则涵盖了各种情况。如果你从许多投资者那里融资，随着他们说“是”就逐步累计。当你开始觉得你已经融够了，可接受的阈值就会开始提高。在实践中，要约存在于一段时间内，而不是一个时间点。所以当你得到一个可接受但与其他要约不相容的要约时（例如，一个愿意投资你所需大部分资金的要约），你可以告诉你正在谈的其他投资者你有一个足够好的要约可以接受，给他们几天时间做出自己的决定。这可能会让你失去一些如果有更多时间可能会做出要约的投资者。但根据定义，你不在乎；初始要约是可接受的。有些投资者会试图通过给你一个“爆炸性”要约来阻止其他人有时间决定，意思是只在几天内有效。来自最优秀投资者的要约爆炸的频率更低、速度更慢——例如，Fred Wilson 从不给爆炸性要约——因为他们相信你会选择他们。但较低层次的投资者有时会给期限非常短的要约，因为他们相信没有其他选择的人会选择他们。三个工作日的期限是可以接受的。如果你一直在并行地与投资者交谈，你不需要更多时间。但更短的期限表明你面对的是一个不靠谱的投资者。你通常可以识破他们的虚张声势，而且你可能需要这样做。【22】

It might seem that instead of accepting offers greedily, your goal should be to get the best investors as partners. That is certainly a good goal, but in phase 2 “get the best investors” only rarely conflicts with “accept offers greedily,” because the best investors don’t usually take any longer to decide than the others. The only case where the two strategies give conflicting advice is when you have to forgo an offer from an acceptable investor to see if you’ll get an offer from a better one. If you talk to investors in parallel and push back on exploding offers with excessively short deadlines, that will almost never happen. But if it does, “get the best investors” is in the average case bad advice. The best investors are also the most selective, because they get their pick of all the startups. They reject nearly everyone they talk to, which means in the average case it’s a bad trade to exchange a definite offer from an acceptable investor for a potential offer from a better one. (The situation is different in phase 1. You can’t apply to all the incubators in parallel, because some offset their schedules to prevent this. In phase 1, “accept offers greedily” and “get the best investors” do conflict, so if you want to apply to multiple incubators, you should do it in such a way that the ones you want most decide first.)

看起来，与其贪婪地接受要约，你的目标应该是获得最好的投资者作为合作伙伴。那当然是一个好目标，但在第二阶段，“获得最好的投资者”很少与“贪婪地接受要约”冲突，因为最好的投资者通常不会比其他投资者花更长的时间做决定。唯一两种策略给出冲突建议的情况是，当你不得不放弃一个可接受投资者的要约，以看看是否能从更好的投资者那里获得要约时。如果你并行地与投资者交谈，并反击期限过短的爆炸性要约，这种情况几乎永远不会发生。但如果确实发生了，“获得最好的投资者”在一般情况下是坏建议。最好的投资者也是最具选择性的，因为他们可以挑选所有初创公司。他们拒绝他们交谈过的几乎所有人，这意味着在一般情况下，用来自可接受投资者的确定要约换取来自更好投资者的潜在要约是一笔糟糕的交易。（情况在第一阶段不同。你不能并行申请所有孵化器，因为有些孵化器会错开他们的时间表来防止这一点。在第一阶段，“贪婪地接受要约”和“获得最好的投资者”确实冲突，所以如果你想申请多个孵化器，你应该以这样一种方式来做，让你最想要的那些先做决定。）

Sometimes when you’re raising money from multiple investors, a series A will emerge out of those conversations, and these rules even cover what to do in that case. When an investor starts to talk to you about a series A, keep taking smaller investments till they actually give you a termsheet. There’s no practical difficulty. If the smaller investments are on convertible notes, they’ll just convert into the series A round. The series A investor won’t like having all these other random investors as bedfellows, but if it bothers them so much they should get on with giving you a termsheet. Till they do, you don’t know for sure they will, and the greedy algorithm tells you what to do. [23]

有时当你从多个投资者那里融资时，在这些对话中会出现一轮 A 轮，这些规则甚至涵盖了那种情况。当一位投资者开始和你谈 A 轮时，继续接受较小的投资，直到他们真正给你一份投资意向书。没有实际困难。如果较小的投资是可转换债券，它们会直接转换为 A 轮。A 轮投资者不会喜欢有所有这些随机的其他投资者作为共同投资者，但如果这让他们如此困扰，他们应该赶紧给你一份投资意向书。在他们这样做之前，你不能确定他们会，而贪婪算法告诉了你该怎么做。【23】

Don’t sell more than 25% in phase 2.

在第二阶段不要出售超过 25%。

If you do well, you will probably raise a series A round eventually. I say probably because things are changing with series A rounds. Startups may start to skip them. But only one company we’ve funded has so far, so tentatively assume the path to huge passes through an A round. [24] Which means you should avoid doing things in earlier rounds that will mess up raising an A round. For example, if you’ve sold more than about 40% of your company total, it starts to get harder to raise an A round, because VCs worry there will not be enough stock left to keep the founders motivated. Our rule of thumb is not to sell more than 25% in phase 2, on top of whatever you sold in phase 1, which should be less than 15%. If you’re raising money on uncapped notes, you’ll have to guess what the eventual equity round valuation might be. Guess conservatively. (Since the goal of this rule is to avoid messing up the series A, there’s obviously an exception if you end up raising a series A in phase 2, as a handful of startups do.)

如果你做得好，你最终可能会进行一轮 A 轮融资。我说可能，因为 A 轮的情况正在变化。初创公司可能开始跳过它们。但到目前为止我们资助的公司中只有一家这样做了，所以暂时假设通往巨大的路径需要通过 A 轮。【24】这意味着你应该避免在早期轮次中做那些会搞砸 A 轮融资的事情。例如，如果你总共出售了公司超过 40% 的股份，就开始更难进行 A 轮融资了，因为风投担心没有足够的股份剩余来保持创始人的积极性。我们的经验法则是，在第二阶段出售的股份加上第一阶段出售的股份（应少于 15%）总共不要超过 25%。如果你在无上限债券上融资，你将不得不猜测最终股权轮次的估值可能是什么。保守地猜测。（由于这条规则的目的是避免搞砸 A 轮，显然如果你最终在第二阶段进行了 A 轮融资，那就有例外——少数初创公司会这样做。）

Have one person handle fundraising.

由一个人处理融资。

If you have multiple founders, pick one to handle fundraising so the other(s) can keep working on the company. And since the danger of fundraising is not the time taken up by the actual meetings but that it becomes the top idea in your mind, the founder who handles fundraising should make a conscious effort to insulate the other founder(s) from the details of the process. [25] (If the founders mistrust one another, this could cause some friction. But if the founders mistrust one another, you have worse problems to worry about than how to organize fundraising.) The founder who handles fundraising should be the CEO, who should in turn be the most formidable of the founders. Even if the CEO is a programmer and another founder is a salesperson? Yes. If you happen to be that type of founding team, you’re effectively a single founder when it comes to fundraising. It’s ok to bring all the founders to meet an investor who will invest a lot, and who needs this meeting as the final step before deciding. But wait till that point. Introducing an investor to your cofounder(s) should be like introducing a girl/boyfriend to your parents — something you do only when things reach a certain stage of seriousness. Even if there are still one or more founders focusing on the company during fundraising, growth will slow. But try to get as much growth as you can, because fundraising is a segment of time, not a point, and what happens to the company during that time affects the outcome. If your numbers grow significantly between two investor meetings, investors will be hot to close, and if your numbers are flat or down they’ll start to get cold feet.

如果你有多个创始人，选择一个来处理融资，这样其他人可以继续在公司工作。而且由于融资的危险不在于实际会议花费的时间，而在于它成为你脑海中的首要想法，负责融资的创始人应该有意识地努力让其他创始人免受过程细节的影响。【25】（如果创始人之间互不信任，这可能会引起一些摩擦。但如果创始人互不信任，你有比如何组织融资更严重的问题要担心。）负责融资的创始人应该是 CEO，而 CEO 又应该是创始人中最强大的那一个。即使 CEO 是程序员而另一位创始人是销售人员？是的。如果你恰好是那种创始团队，在融资方面你实际上相当于一个单一创始人。把所有创始人都带去见一位会投资很多、并且需要这次会面作为决定前最后一步的投资者是可以的。但要等到那个时候。把投资者介绍给你的联合创始人应该像把男/女朋友介绍给你的父母一样——只有在事情达到一定严肃程度时才做。即使在融资期间还有一位或更多的创始人专注于公司，增长也会放缓。但要尽量获得尽可能多的增长，因为融资是一个时间段，而不是一个时间点，公司在那段时间内发生的情况会影响结果。如果你的数字在两次投资者会议之间有显著增长，投资者会急于成交，如果你的数字持平或下降，他们就会开始退缩。

You’ll need an executive summary and (maybe) a deck.

你需要一份执行摘要和（也许）一份演示文稿。

Traditionally phase 2 fundraising consists of presenting a slide deck in person to investors. Sequoia describes what such a deck should contain, and since they’re the customer you can take their word for it. I say “traditionally” because I’m ambivalent about decks, and (though perhaps this is wishful thinking) they seem to be on the way out. A lot of the most successful startups we fund never make decks in phase 2. They just talk to investors and explain what they plan to do. Fundraising usually takes off fast for the startups that are most successful at it, and they’re thus able to excuse themselves by saying that they haven’t had time to make a deck. You’ll also want an executive summary, which should be no more than a page long and describe in the most matter of fact language what you plan to do, why it’s a good idea, and what progress you’ve made so far. The point of the summary is to remind the investor (who may have met many startups that day) what you talked about. Assume that if you give someone a copy of your deck or executive summary, it will be passed on to whoever you’d least like to have it. But don’t refuse on that account to give copies to investors you meet. You just have to treat such leaks as a cost of doing business. In practice it’s not that high a cost. Though founders are rightly indignant when their plans get leaked to competitors, I can’t think of a startup whose outcome has been affected by it. Sometimes an investor will ask you to send them your deck and/or executive summary before they decide whether to meet with you. I wouldn’t do that. It’s a sign they’re not really interested.

传统上，第二阶段的融资包括亲自向投资者展示幻灯片演示文稿。Sequoia 描述了这样的演示文稿应该包含什么，既然他们是客户，你可以相信他们的话。我说“传统上”是因为我对演示文稿持矛盾态度，而且（尽管这可能是一厢情愿）它们似乎正在过时。我们资助的许多最成功的初创公司在第二阶段从不制作演示文稿。他们只是与投资者交谈并解释他们计划做什么。对于在融资方面最成功的初创公司来说，融资通常进展很快，因此他们可以以没有时间制作演示文稿为由推脱。你还需要一份执行摘要，不应超过一页，用最实事求是的语言描述你计划做什么，为什么这是一个好主意，以及到目前为止你取得了什么进展。摘要的目的是提醒投资者（他们那天可能见了许多初创公司）你们谈了什么。假设如果你给某人一份你的演示文稿或执行摘要的副本，它会被传给你最不希望拥有它的人。但不要因此拒绝给你见面的投资者副本。你只能把这种泄露当作做生意的成本。在实践中，成本并不那么高。尽管创始人的计划被泄露给竞争对手时他们有理由感到愤慨，但我想不出有哪家初创公司的结果受到了影响。有时投资者会要求你在决定是否与你见面之前，先把你的演示文稿和/或执行摘要发给他们。我不会那样做。这表明他们并不是真的感兴趣。

Stop fundraising when it stops working.

当融资不再有效时停止融资。

When do you stop fundraising? Ideally when you’ve raised enough. But what if you haven’t raised as much as you’d like? When do you give up? It’s hard to give general advice about this, because there have been cases of startups that kept trying to raise money even when it seemed hopeless, and miraculously succeeded. But what I usually tell founders is to stop fundraising when you start to get a lot of air in the straw. When you’re drinking through a straw, you can tell when you get to the end of the liquid because you start to get a lot of air in the straw. When your fundraising options run out, they usually run out in the same way. Don’t keep sucking on the straw if you’re just getting air. It’s not going to get better.

你什么时候停止融资？理想情况下，当你融够了的时候。但如果你没有融到想要的数量呢？你什么时候放弃？对此很难给出一般性建议，因为确实有初创公司在融资看起来无望时继续尝试，并奇迹般地成功了的案例。但我通常告诉创始人的是，当你开始吸到大量空气时停止融资。当你用吸管喝饮料时，你可以判断什么时候到了液体的尽头，因为你开始吸到大量空气。当你的融资选择耗尽时，它们通常以同样的方式耗尽。如果你只是在吸空气，就不要继续吸了。情况不会变好。

Don’t get addicted to fundraising.

不要对融资上瘾。

Fundraising is a chore for most founders, but some find it more interesting than working on their startup. The work at an early stage startup often consists of unglamorous schleps. Whereas fundraising, when it’s going well, can be quite the opposite. Instead of sitting in your grubby apartment listening to users complain about bugs in your software, you’re being offered millions of dollars by famous investors over lunch at a nice restaurant. [26] The danger of fundraising is particularly acute for people who are good at it. It’s always fun to work on something you’re good at. If you’re one of these people, beware. Fundraising is not what will make your company successful. Listening to users complain about bugs in your software is what will make you successful. And the big danger of getting addicted to fundraising is not merely that you’ll spend too long on it or raise too much money. It’s that you’ll start to think of yourself as being already successful, and lose your taste for the schleps you need to undertake to actually be successful. Startups can be destroyed by this. When I see a startup with young founders that is fabulously successful at fundraising, I mentally decrease my estimate of the probability that they’ll succeed. The press may be writing about them as if they’d been anointed as the next Google, but I’m thinking “this is going to end badly.”

对大多数创始人来说，融资是一件苦差事，但有些人觉得它比做初创公司更有趣。早期初创公司的工作通常包括不光彩的脏活累活。而融资，当它进展顺利时，可能恰恰相反。你不是坐在你邋遢的公寓里听用户抱怨你软件中的 bug，而是在一家不错的餐厅吃午餐时，被知名投资者提供数百万美元。【26】融资的危险对那些擅长它的人来说尤其严重。做你擅长的事情总是很有趣。如果你是这些人之一，要当心。融资不会让你的公司成功。倾听用户抱怨你软件中的 bug 才会让你成功。而对融资上瘾的大危险不仅仅在于你会花太长时间或融太多钱。而是你会开始认为你已经成功了，并失去对那些你为了真正成功而需要做的脏活累活的兴趣。初创公司可能会因此被摧毁。当我看到一家年轻创始人的初创公司在融资方面取得了惊人的成功时，我会在心里降低他们成功的概率。媒体可能会写着好像他们已经被加冕为下一个 Google，但我在想“这将会以糟糕的方式结束。”

Don’t raise too much.

不要融太多钱。

Though only a handful of startups have to worry about this, it is possible to raise too much. The dangers of raising too much are subtle but insidious. One is that it will set impossibly high expectations. If you raise an excessive amount of money, it will be at a high valuation, and the danger of raising money at too high a valuation is that you won’t be able to increase it sufficiently the next time you raise money. A company’s valuation is expected to rise each time it raises money. If not it’s a sign of a company in trouble, which makes you unattractive to investors. So if you raise money in phase 2 at a post-money valuation of $30 million, the pre-money valuation of your next round, if you want to raise one, is going to have to be at least $50 million. And you have to be doing really, really well to raise money at $50 million. It’s very dangerous to let the competitiveness of your current round set the performance threshold you have to meet to raise your next one, because the two are only loosely coupled. But the money itself may be more dangerous than the valuation. The more you raise, the more you spend, and spending a lot of money can be disastrous for an early stage startup. Spending a lot makes it harder to become profitable, and perhaps even worse, it makes you more rigid, because the main way to spend money is people, and the more people you have, the harder it is to change directions. So if you do raise a huge amount of money, don’t spend it. (You will find that advice almost impossible to follow, so hot will be the money burning a hole in your pocket, but I feel obliged at least to try.)

虽然只有少数初创公司需要担心这个问题，但融太多钱是可能的。融太多钱的危险是微妙但阴险的。一个是它会设定高得不可能的期望。如果你融了过多的钱，那会是在高估值下，而以过高估值融资的危险是，下次融资时你无法足够地提高它。一家公司的估值预计每次融资都会上升。如果不是，那就是公司遇到麻烦的信号，这会使你对投资者失去吸引力。所以如果你在第二阶段以 3000 万美元的融资后估值融资，那么如果你想要进行下一轮融资，下一轮的融资前估值将至少需要达到 5000 万美元。而你必须做得非常非常好才能以 5000 万美元融资。让你当前轮次的竞争性设定你需要达到的绩效门槛来启动下一轮是非常危险的，因为这两者只是松散耦合的。但金钱本身可能比估值更危险。你融得越多，你花得越多，而花很多钱对早期初创公司来说可能是灾难性的。花很多钱使你更难实现盈利，甚至更糟的是，它使你更僵化，因为花钱的主要方式是雇人，而人越多，改变方向就越难。所以如果你确实融了一大笔钱，不要花掉它。（你会发现这个建议几乎不可能遵循，因为钱会烧穿你的口袋，但我感到有义务至少尝试一下。）

Be nice.

要友善。

Startups raising money occasionally alienate investors by seeming arrogant. Sometimes because they are arrogant, and sometimes because they’re noobs clumsily attempting to mimic the toughness they’ve observed in experienced founders. It’s a mistake to behave arrogantly to investors. While there are certain situations in which certain investors like certain kinds of arrogance, investors vary greatly in this respect, and a flick of the whip that will bring one to heel will make another roar with indignation. The only safe strategy is never to seem arrogant at all. That will require some diplomacy if you follow the advice I’ve given here, because the advice I’ve given is essentially how to play hardball back. When you refuse to meet an investor because you’re not in fundraising mode, or slow down your interactions with an investor who moves too slow, or treat a contingent offer as the no it actually is and then, by accepting offers greedily, end up leaving that investor out, you’re going to be doing things investors don’t like. So you must cushion the blow with soft words. At YC we tell startups they can blame us. And now that I’ve written this, everyone else can blame me if they want. That plus the inexperience card should work in most situations: sorry, we think you’re great, but PG said startups shouldn’t __ , and since we’re new to fundraising, we feel like we have to play it safe. The danger of behaving arrogantly is greatest when you’re doing well. When everyone wants you, it’s hard not to let it go to your head. Especially if till recently no one wanted you. But restrain yourself. The startup world is a small place, and startups have lots of ups and downs. This is a domain where it’s more true than usual that pride goeth before a fall. [27]

融资中的初创公司有时会因为显得傲慢而疏远投资者。有时是因为他们确实傲慢，有时是因为他们是新手，笨拙地试图模仿他们在经验丰富的创始人身上观察到的强硬态度。对投资者表现得傲慢是错误的。虽然在某些情况下某些投资者喜欢某种类型的傲慢，但投资者在这方面差异很大，一记鞭子可能会让一个人顺从，却会让另一个人愤怒地咆哮。唯一安全的策略是永远不要显得傲慢。如果你遵循我在这里给出的建议，那将需要一些外交手腕，因为我给出的建议本质上是如何打硬球。当你因为你不在融资模式而拒绝见一位投资者，或者放慢与行动太慢的投资者的互动，或者把一个附带条件的要约当作它实际上是的“不”来对待，然后通过贪婪地接受要约最终把那个投资者排除在外时，你将会做投资者不喜欢的事情。所以你必须用温和的话语来缓冲打击。在 YC，我们告诉初创公司他们可以怪我们。既然我写了这篇文章，其他人如果愿意也可以怪我。那加上新手牌在大多数情况下应该有效：对不起，我们认为你们很棒，但 PG 说初创公司不应该 ___ ，而且因为我们是融资新手，我们觉得我们必须稳妥行事。表现得傲慢的危险在你做得好的时候最大。当每个人都想要你的时候，很难不让它冲昏头脑。特别是如果直到不久前还没有人想要你。但要克制自己。初创世界是一个小地方，初创公司有很多起起落落。在这个领域，“骄傲在败坏以先”比平常更真实。【27】

Be nice when investors reject you as well. The best investors are not wedded to their initial opinion of you. If they reject you in phase 2 and you end up doing well, they’ll often invest in phase 3. In fact investors who reject you are some of your warmest leads for future fundraising. Any investor who spent significant time deciding probably came close to saying yes. Often you have some internal champion who only needs a little more evidence to convince the skeptics. So it’s wise not merely to be nice to investors who reject you, but (unless they behaved badly) to treat it as the beginning of a relationship.

当投资者拒绝你时也要友善。最好的投资者不会固守他们对你的初始看法。如果他们在第二阶段拒绝你，而你最终做得很好，他们通常会在第三阶段投资。事实上，拒绝你的投资者是你未来融资的一些最温暖的线索。任何花了大量时间做决定的投资者可能都接近说“是”。通常你有一些内部支持者，只需要多一点证据就能说服怀疑者。所以明智的做法不仅是对拒绝你的投资者友善，而且（除非他们行为不当）把它当作一段关系的开始。

The bar will be higher next time.

下一次门槛会更高。

Assume the money you raise in phase 2 will be the last you ever raise. You must make it to profitability on this money if you can. Over the past several years, the investment community has evolved from a strategy of anointing a small number of winners early and then supporting them for years to a strategy of spraying money at early stage startups and then ruthlessly culling them at the next stage. This is probably the optimal strategy for investors. It’s too hard to pick winners early on. Better to let the market do it for you. But it often comes as a surprise to startups how much harder it is to raise money in phase 3. When your company is only a couple months old, all it has to be is a promising experiment that’s worth funding to see how it turns out. The next time you raise money, the experiment has to have worked. You have to be on a trajectory that leads to going public. And while there are some ideas where the proof that the experiment worked might consist of e.g. query response times, usually the proof is profitability. Usually phase 3 fundraising has to be type A fundraising. In practice there are two ways startups hose themselves between phases 2 and 3. Some are just too slow to become profitable. They raise enough money to last for two years. There doesn’t seem any particular urgency to be profitable. So they don’t make any effort to make money for a year. But by that time, not making money has become habitual. When they finally decide to try, they find they can’t. The other way companies hose themselves is by letting their expenses grow too fast. Which almost always means hiring too many people. You usually shouldn’t go out and hire 8 people as soon as you raise money at phase 2. Usually you want to wait till you have growth (and thus usually revenues) to justify them. A lot of VCs will encourage you to hire aggressively. VCs generally tell you to spend too much, partly because as money people they err on the side of solving problems by spending money, and partly because they want you to sell them more of your company in subsequent rounds. Don’t listen to them.

假设你在第二阶段融到的钱将是你最后融到的钱。你必须尽可能用这笔钱实现盈利。在过去几年中，投资界已经从一种早期指定少数赢家然后支持他们多年的策略，演变为一种向早期初创公司撒钱然后在下一阶段无情淘汰的策略。这对投资者来说可能是最优策略。早期挑选赢家太难了。最好让市场为你做这件事。但初创公司常常惊讶于在第三阶段融资要困难得多。当你的公司只有几个月大时，它只需要是一个有前途的实验，值得投资看看结果如何。下次你融资时，实验必须已经成功了。你必须处在一个通往上市的轨道上。虽然有些实验中证明实验成功的证据可能是例如查询响应时间，但通常证明是盈利能力。通常第三阶段融资必须是 A 类融资。在实践中，初创公司在第二阶段和第三阶段之间搞砸自己的方式有两种。有些只是太慢而无法实现盈利。他们融了足够维持两年的钱。似乎没有什么特别的紧迫性去盈利。所以他们一年都没有任何赚钱的努力。但到那时，不赚钱已经成了习惯。当他们最终决定尝试时，他们发现自己做不到。公司搞砸自己的另一种方式是让开支增长太快。这几乎总是意味着雇了太多人。你通常不应该在第二阶段融资后就出去雇 8 个人。通常你想等到有了增长（因此通常有收入）来证明他们的合理性。很多风投会鼓励你积极招聘。风投通常告诉你要花太多钱，部分原因是作为金融人士，他们倾向于通过花钱来解决问题，部分原因是他们希望你在后续轮次中卖给他们更多的公司股份。不要听他们的。

Don’t make things complicated.

不要把事情搞复杂。

I realize it may seem odd to sum up this huge treatise by saying that my overall advice is not to make fundraising too complicated, but if you go back and look at this list you’ll see it’s basically a simple recipe with a lot of implications and edge cases. Avoid investors till you decide to raise money, and then when you do, talk to them all in parallel, prioritized by expected value, and accept offers greedily. That’s fundraising in one sentence. Don’t introduce complicated optimizations, and don’t let investors introduce complications either. Fundraising is not what will make you successful. It’s just a means to an end. Your primary goal should be to get it over with and get back to what will make you successful — making things and talking to users — and the path I’ve described will for most startups be the surest way to that destination.

我意识到用“我总体的建议是不要把融资搞得太复杂”来总结这篇长篇大论可能看起来很奇怪，但如果你回头看看这份清单，你会看到它基本上是一个简单的配方，有很多含义和边缘情况。在你决定融资之前避开投资者，然后当你决定融资时，并行地与他们所有人交谈，按期望值优先排序，并贪婪地接受要约。这就是一句话的融资。不要引入复杂的优化，也不要让投资者引入复杂化。融资不会让你成功。它只是达到目的的一种手段。你的首要目标应该是尽快完成它，然后回到那些会让你成功的事情上——做产品和与用户交流——而我所描述的路径对大多数初创公司来说将是通往那个目的地的最可靠的方式。

Be good, take care of yourselves, and don’t leave the path.

做好，照顾好自己，不要偏离路径。

注释

[1] The worst explosions happen when unpromising-seeming startups encounter mediocre investors. Good investors don’t lead startups on; their reputations are too valuable. And startups that seem promising can usually get enough money from good investors that they don’t have to talk to mediocre ones. It is the unpromising-seeming startups that have to resort to raising money from mediocre investors. And it’s particularly damaging when these investors flake, because unpromising-seeming startups are usually more desperate for money. (Not all unpromising-seeming startups do badly. Some are merely ugly ducklings in the sense that they violate current startup fashions.)

【1】最严重的爆炸发生在看起来不太有前途的初创公司遇到平庸投资者的时候。好的投资者不会吊着初创公司；他们的声誉太宝贵了。而且看起来有前途的初创公司通常可以从好投资者那里获得足够的钱，不必与平庸的投资者打交道。是那些看起来不太有前途的初创公司不得不诉诸于从平庸投资者那里融资。而当这些投资者不靠谱时，尤其具有破坏性，因为看起来不太有前途的初创公司通常更迫切需要钱。（并非所有看起来不太有前途的初创公司都表现不佳。有些仅仅是丑小鸭，意思是它们违背了当前的初创公司时尚。）

[2] One YC founder told me: I think in general we’ve done ok at fundraising, but I managed to screw up twice at the exact same thing — trying to focus on building the company and fundraising at the same time.

【2】一位 YC 创始人告诉我：我认为总的来说我们在融资方面做得还可以，但我在完全相同的两件事上搞砸了——试图同时专注于建立公司和融资。

[3] There is one subtle danger you have to watch out for here, which I warn about later: beware of getting too high a valuation from an eager investor, lest that set an impossibly high target when raising additional money.

【3】这里有一个微妙的危险你必须注意，我稍后会警告：小心从过于急切的投资者那里获得过高的估值，以免在融更多钱时设定一个高得不可能的目标。

[4] If they really need a meeting, then they’re not ready to invest, regardless of what they say. They’re still deciding, which means you’re being asked to come in and convince them. Which is fundraising.

【4】如果他们真的需要一次会面，那么无论他们说什么，他们都没有准备好投资。他们还在决定，这意味着你被要求来见他们并说服他们。那就是融资。

[5] Associates at VC firms regularly cold email startups. Naive founders think “Wow, a VC is interested in us!” But an associate is not a VC. They have no decision-making power. And while they may introduce startups they like to partners at their firm, the partners discriminate against deals that come to them this way. I don’t know of a single VC investment that began with an associate cold-emailing a startup. If you want to approach a specific firm, get an intro to a partner from someone they respect. It’s ok to talk to an associate if you get an intro to a VC firm or they see you at a Demo Day and they begin by having an associate vet you. That’s not a promising lead and should therefore get low priority, but it’s not as completely worthless as a cold email. Because the title “associate” has gotten a bad reputation, a few VC firms have started to give their associates the title “partner,” which can make things very confusing. If you’re a YC startup you can ask us who’s who; otherwise you may have to do some research online. There may be a special title for actual partners. If someone speaks for the firm in the press or a blog on the firm’s site, they’re probably a real partner. If they’re on boards of directors they’re probably a real partner. There are titles between “associate” and “partner,” including “principal” and “venture partner.” The meanings of these titles vary too much to generalize.

【5】风投公司的合伙人经常给初创公司发冷邮件。天真的创始人想“哇，一家风投对我们感兴趣！”但合伙人不是风投。他们没有决策权。虽然他们可能会把他们喜欢的初创公司介绍给他们公司的合伙人，但合伙人会歧视以这种方式来到他们面前的交易。我不知道有任何风投投资是从合伙人给初创公司发冷邮件开始的。如果你想接触某家公司，从他们尊重的人那里获得向合伙人的引荐。如果你获得了一家风投公司的引荐，或者他们在 Demo Day 上看到你并且他们开始让合伙人先与你接洽，那与合伙人谈话是可以的。那不是一个有前途的线索，因此应该得到低优先级，但不像冷邮件那样完全无价值。因为“合伙人”这个头衔已经有了不好的名声，一些风投公司开始给他们的合伙人“合伙人”的头衔，这可能会让事情变得非常混乱。如果你是一家 YC 初创公司，你可以问我们谁是谁；否则你可能需要在网上做一些研究。对于真正的合伙人可能有特殊的头衔。如果有人在媒体或公司网站上的博客中代表公司发言，他们很可能是真正的合伙人。如果他们在董事会中，他们很可能是真正的合伙人。在“合伙人”和“合伙人”之间有头衔，包括“负责人”和“风险合伙人”。这些头衔的含义变化太大，无法一概而论。

[6] For similar reasons, avoid casual conversations with potential acquirers. They can lead to distractions even more dangerous than fundraising. Don’t even take a meeting with a potential acquirer unless you want to sell your company right now.

【6】出于类似的原因，避免与潜在收购者进行随意的对话。它们可能导致比融资更危险的分散注意力。除非你现在就想卖掉你的公司，否则甚至不要与潜在收购者会面。

[7] Joshua Reeves specifically suggests asking each investor to intro you to two more investors. Don’t ask investors who say no for introductions to other investors. That will in many cases be an anti-recommendation.

【7】Joshua Reeves 特别建议让每个投资者把你介绍给另外两个投资者。不要要求拒绝你的投资者向其他投资者介绍你。这在许多情况下会是一个反推荐。

[8] This is not always as deliberate as its sounds. A lot of the delays and disconnects between founders and investors are induced by the customs of the venture business, which have evolved the way they have because they suit investors’ interests.

【8】这并不总是听起来那么刻意。创始人和投资者之间的许多延迟和脱节是由风险投资行业的惯例引起的，这些惯例以它们的方式演变是因为它们符合投资者的利益。

[9] One YC founder who read a draft of this essay wrote: This is the most important section. I think it might bear stating even more clearly. “Investors will deliberately affect more interest than they have to preserve optionality. If an investor seems very interested in you, they still probably won’t invest. The solution for this is to assume the worst — that an investor is just feigning interest — until you get a definite commitment.”

【9】一位阅读了本文草稿的 YC 创始人写道：这是最重要的部分。我认为可能需要更明确地说明。“投资者会故意表现出比实际更多的兴趣来保持选择权。如果一位投资者似乎对你非常感兴趣，他们仍然可能不会投资。解决这个问题的方法是假设最坏的情况——即投资者只是假装感兴趣——直到你得到一个明确的承诺。”

[10] Though you should probably pack investor meetings as closely as you can, Jeff Byun mentions one reason not to: if you pack investor meetings too closely, you’ll have less time for your pitch to evolve. Some founders deliberately schedule a handful of lame investors first, to get the bugs out of their pitch.

【10】虽然你应该尽可能紧凑地安排投资者会议，但 Jeff Byun 提到了一个不这样做的原因：如果你把投资者会议安排得太紧凑，你将没有太多时间让你的推销进化。一些创始人故意先安排一些不太重要的投资者，以排除他们推销中的问题。

[11] There is not an efficient market in this respect. Some of the most useless investors are also the highest maintenance.

【11】在这方面并不存在有效的市场。一些最无用的投资者也是维护成本最高的。

[12] Incidentally, this paragraph is sales 101. If you want to see it in action, go talk to a car dealer.

【12】顺便说一句，这一段是销售 101。如果你想看它在实际中的运作，去和汽车经销商谈谈。

[13] I know one very smooth founder who used to end investor meetings with “So, can I count you in?” delivered as if it were “Can you pass the salt?” Unless you’re very smooth (if you’re not sure…), do not do this yourself. There is nothing more unconvincing, for an investor, than a nerdy founder trying to deliver the lines meant for a smooth one. Investors are fine with funding nerds. So if you’re a nerd, just try to be a good nerd, rather than doing a bad imitation of a smooth salesman.

【13】我知道一位非常老练的创始人，他曾经在投资者会议结束时说“那么，我能把你算进来吗？”说得好像是在说“你能把盐递过来吗？”除非你非常老练（如果你不确定……），不要自己这样做。对一个投资者来说，没有什么比一个书呆子创始人试图说出属于老练销售人员的台词更不令人信服了。投资者完全愿意资助书呆子。所以如果你是一个书呆子，只要努力做一个好的书呆子，而不是糟糕地模仿一个老练的销售人员。

[14] Ian Hogarth suggests a good way to tell how serious potential investors are: the resources they expend on you after the first meeting. An investor who’s seriously interested will already be working to help you even before they’ve committed.

【14】Ian Hogarth 建议了一个判断潜在投资者有多认真的好方法：他们在第一次会议后在你身上投入的资源。一个真正感兴趣的投资者甚至在承诺之前就已经在努力帮助你了。

[15] In principle you might have to think about so-called “signalling risk.” If a prestigious VC makes a small seed investment in you, what if they don’t want to invest the next time you raise money? Other investors might assume that the VC knows you well, since they’re an existing investor, and if they don’t want to invest in your next round, that must mean you suck. The reason I say “in principle” is that in practice signalling hasn’t been much of a problem so far. It rarely arises, and in the few cases where it does, the startup in question usually is doing badly and is doomed anyway. If you have the luxury of choosing among seed investors, you can play it safe by excluding VC firms. But it isn’t critical to.

【15】原则上你可能需要考虑所谓的“信号风险”。如果一家有声望的风投对你进行了小额种子投资，但他们不想在你下次融资时投资呢？其他投资者可能会假设那家风投很了解你，因为他们是现有投资者，如果他们不想投资你的下一轮，那一定意味着你很糟糕。我说“原则上”是因为在实践中信号风险到目前为止还没有成为太大的问题。它很少出现，而在少数出现的情况下，相关的初创公司通常表现不佳并且无论如何都注定失败。如果你有幸在种子投资者中有选择，你可以通过排除风投公司来安全行事。但这并不是关键。

[16] Sometimes a competitor will deliberately threaten you with a lawsuit just as you start fundraising, because they know you’ll have to disclose the threat to potential investors and they hope this will make it harder for you to raise money. If this happens it will probably frighten you more than investors. Experienced investors know about this trick, and know the actual lawsuits rarely happen. So if you’re attacked in this way, be forthright with investors. They’ll be more alarmed if you seem evasive than if you tell them everything.

【16】有时竞争对手会在你刚开始融资时故意用诉讼威胁你，因为他们知道你必须向潜在投资者披露这一威胁，他们希望这会让你更难融资。如果发生这种情况，它可能会让你比投资者更害怕。有经验的投资者知道这种伎俩，也知道实际的诉讼很少发生。所以如果你以这种方式受到攻击，对投资者要坦诚。如果你看起来躲躲闪闪，比如果你告诉他们一切，他们会更警觉。

[17] A related trick is to claim that they’ll only invest contingently on other investors doing so because otherwise you’d be “undercapitalized.” This is almost always bullshit. They can’t estimate your minimum capital needs that precisely.

【17】一个相关的伎俩是声称他们只会附带条件地投资于其他投资者的投资，因为否则你会“资本不足”。这几乎总是胡说八道。他们无法那么精确地估计你的最低资本需求。

[18] You won’t hire all those 20 people at once, and you’ll probably have some revenues before 18 months are out. But those too are acceptable or at least accepted additions to the margin for error.

【18】你不会一下子雇佣所有这 20 个人，而且在 18 个月结束之前你可能会有一些收入。但这些也是可接受的，或者至少是被接受的误差余量增加项。

[19] Type A fundraising is so much better that it might even be worth doing something different if it gets you there sooner. One YC founder told me that if he were a first-time founder again he’d “leave ideas that are up-front capital intensive to founders with established reputations.”

【19】A 类融资如此之好，以至于如果做点不同的事情能让你更快到达那里，那甚至可能是值得的。一位 YC 创始人告诉我，如果他再次成为首次创始人，他会“把前期资本密集型的想法留给有既定声誉的创始人。”

[20] I don’t know whether this happens because they’re innumerate, or because they believe they have zero ability to predict startup outcomes (in which case this behavior at least wouldn’t be irrational). In either case the implications are similar.

【20】我不知道这是因为他们不识数，还是因为他们相信他们没有能力预测初创公司的结果（在这种情况下，这种行为至少不会是 irrational）。无论哪种情况，影响都是相似的。

[21] If you’re a YC startup and you have an investor who for some reason insists that you decide the price, any YC partner can estimate a market price for you.

【21】如果你是一家 YC 初创公司，并且你有一位投资者出于某种原因坚持让你决定价格，任何 YC 合伙人都可以为你估算一个市场价格。

[22] You should respond in kind when investors behave upstandingly too. When an investor makes you a clean offer with no deadline, you have a moral obligation to respond promptly.

【22】当投资者行为正派时，你也应该以同样的方式回应。当一位投资者给你一个没有截止日期的干净要约时，你有道德义务及时回应。

[23] Tell the investors talking to you about an A round about the smaller investments you raise as you raise them. You owe them such updates on your cap table, and this is also a good way to pressure them to act. They won’t like you raising other money and may pressure you to stop, but they can’t legitimately ask you to commit to them till they also commit to you. If they want you to stop raising money, the way to do it is to give you a series A termsheet with a no-shop clause. You can relent a little if the potential series A investor has a great reputation and they’re clearly working fast to get you a termsheet, particularly if a third party like YC is involved to ensure there are no misunderstandings. But be careful.

【23】告诉那些正在和你谈 A 轮的投资者你在融资过程中进行的小额投资。你有义务向他们更新你的股权结构表，这也是给他们施压行动的好方法。他们不会喜欢你融其他钱，可能会给你施压让你停止，但他们不能合法地要求你在他们也承诺你之前就承诺他们。如果他们想让你停止融资，方法是给你一份带有“禁止询价”条款的 A 轮投资意向书。如果潜在的 A 轮投资者声誉很好，并且他们明显在快速工作以给你一份投资意向书，特别是如果像 YC 这样的第三方参与以确保没有误解，你可以稍微让步。但要小心。

[24] The company is Weebly, which made it to profitability on a seed investment of $650k. They did try to raise a series A in the fall of 2008 but (no doubt partly because it was the fall of 2008) the terms they were offered were so bad that they decided to skip raising an A round.

【24】这家公司是 Weebly，它在 65 万美元的种子投资上实现了盈利。他们在 2008 年秋天确实试图进行 A 轮融资，但（毫无疑问部分因为那是 2008 年秋天）他们被提供的条款太糟糕了，以至于他们决定跳过 A 轮融资。

[25] Another advantage of having one founder take fundraising meetings is that you never have to negotiate in real time, which is something inexperienced founders should avoid. One YC founder told me: Investors are professional negotiators and can negotiate on the spot very easily. If only one founder is in the room, you can say “I need to circle back with my co-founder” before making any commitments. I used to do this all the time.

【25】让一位创始人负责融资会议的另一个好处是，你永远不必实时谈判，这是缺乏经验的创始人应该避免的。一位 YC 创始人告诉我：投资者是专业谈判者，可以非常容易地当场谈判。如果只有一位创始人在房间里，你可以在做出任何承诺之前说“我需要和我的联合创始人商量一下”。我经常这样做。

[26] You’ll be lucky if fundraising feels pleasant enough to become addictive. More often you have to worry about the other extreme — becoming demoralized when investors reject you. As one (very successful) YC founder wrote after reading a draft of this: It’s hard to mentally deal with the sheer scale of rejection in fundraising and if you are not in the right mindset you will fail. Users may love you but these supposedly smart investors may not understand you at all. At this point for me, rejection still rankles but I’ve come to accept that investors are just not super thoughtful for the most part and you need to play the game according to certain somewhat depressing rules (many of which you are listing) in order to win.

【26】如果融资感觉足够愉快以至于让人上瘾，那你就算幸运了。更常见的是，你需要担心另一个极端——当投资者拒绝你时变得士气低落。正如一位（非常成功的）YC 创始人在阅读本文草稿后所写的：在融资中应对如此大规模的拒绝在心理上很难，如果你没有正确的心态，你会失败。用户可能喜欢你，但这些 supposedly 聪明的投资者可能根本不理解你。在这一点上对我来说，拒绝仍然令人恼火，但我已经接受了投资者大多不是特别深思熟虑的，你需要按照某些相当令人沮丧的规则（其中许多你正在列出）来玩游戏才能获胜。

[27] The actual sentence in the King James Bible is “Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall.”

【27】《钦定版圣经》中的实际句子是“骄傲在败坏以先，狂心在跌倒之前。”
